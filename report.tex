\section{Big-M Simplex Method}

The case study is the diet, or feed-blending, problem. A dairy blends two
supplements into 1\,kg batches of cattle feed. Supplement A costs Rs\,4/kg and B
costs Rs\,1/kg. The blend has to hit a protein specification exactly, clear a
vitamin floor, and fit inside the mixing drum.

\subsection{Linear programming formulation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=plain]
x1 = kg of supplement A per batch
x2 = kg of supplement B per batch

minimise    Z = 4*x1 + x2

subject to  3*x1 +  x2  = 3      protein, exact specification
            4*x1 + 3*x2 >= 6     vitamin premix floor
             x1 + 2*x2  <= 4     mixing drum capacity
            x1, x2 >= 0
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Conversion to standard form}
The program adds the variables itself. Each row then owns exactly one basic
variable, which gives the simplex a starting basis. The origin is not feasible
here, which is why the artificial variables are there.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{Variables added}\\
\midrule
\texttt{3*x1 + x2 = 3}     & artificial \texttt{a1}\\
\texttt{4*x1 + 3*x2 >= 6}  & surplus \texttt{e2}, artificial \texttt{a2}\\
\texttt{x1 + 2*x2 <= 4}    & slack \texttt{s3}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The objective penalises each artificial by \texttt{+M}. Instead of
substituting a large number for M, the objective row is held as two parallel
Fraction rows, one for the M coefficients and one for the constants. Comparing
the pair \texttt{(M coefficient, constant)} reproduces the rule that any M term
outweighs any constant. That avoids floating-point error, and it avoids having
to guess a numeric M large enough for the data.

\section{Transportation Problem: VAM and MODI}

The second case study is cement distribution. Three plants ship to four regional
warehouses. Output and requirement are fixed for the month, and freight per
tonne depends on which plant supplies which warehouse.

\subsection{Problem data}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=plain]
capacity (tonnes)  S1 = 20   S2 = 30   S3 = 50            total 100
demand   (tonnes)  D1 = 10   D2 = 40   D3 = 20   D4 = 30  total 100

freight c_ij, Rs per tonne

        D1    D2    D3    D4
  S1     8     6    10     9
  S2     9    12    13     7
  S3    14     9    16     5
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Linear programming formulation}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=plain]
x_ij = tonnes shipped from plant i to warehouse j

minimise    Z = sum_i sum_j  c_ij * x_ij

subject to  sum_j x_ij = a_i    each plant ships its full output
            sum_i x_ij = b_j    each warehouse gets its requirement
            x_ij >= 0
\end{lstlisting}

Supply and demand balance in this case study. When they do not, the program
inserts a zero-cost dummy row or column before solving.

\subsection{Phase 1: Vogel's Approximation Method}
VAM builds the initial basic feasible solution. It finds the row or column with
the largest penalty, meaning the gap between that line's two cheapest cells,
then fills the cheapest cell in that line as far as the remaining supply and
demand allow, repeating until everything is allocated.

\lstinputlisting[style=out]{outputs/vam_output.txt}

\subsection{Phase 2: MODI}
MODI tests that solution for optimality. It solves \texttt{u\_i + v\_j = c\_ij}
across the basic cells, anchored at \texttt{u\_0 = 0}, to get shadow prices. Any
non-basic cell whose opportunity cost \texttt{c\_ij - u\_i - v\_j} is negative
would lower the total, so that cell enters the basis and the allocation shifts
around a closed loop until no negative opportunity cost is left.
